# The Tether

A tether is the line that keeps two things joined when one of them drifts. It isn't decorative. You
mostly don't see it. You notice it the day it isn't there.

Most of what holds two people together is like that. It isn't the anniversary dinner. It's that
they know how you take your coffee and they made it that way this morning without being asked, and
you drank it without looking up.

This journal is ninety days of looking up.

The idea behind it is simple enough to say in a sentence: you already know why you love this
person, and you have almost certainly stopped saying it. Not out of coldness. Out of obviousness.
The reasons became so familiar they went quiet, the way a tether does.

There is some evidence that saying them matters. In a set of studies published in 2012, people who
felt more appreciated by their partners were more appreciative in return — and the more
appreciative partners were also more responsive to what their partner needed, more committed, and
more likely to still be there later [C-001]. That is a correlation, not a promise. It doesn't say
gratitude keeps people together. It says that in couples who last, this particular thing tends to
be present.

Two other findings shaped how this book works.

In 2006, researchers asked one partner in each of eighty-six dating couples to write for twenty
minutes a day, three days running, about their deepest thoughts and feelings about the
relationship. A comparison group wrote about their daily activities. Three months later, the
couples in the writing group were more likely to still be together [C-003]. One study, eighty-six
couples, twenty years ago. Not proof of anything. But it is the reason you write your side alone.

And in 2013, partners who expressed gratitude to each other in a laboratory task — out loud, to the
person's face — showed better relationship quality six months on, and what predicted it was whether
the listener felt *understood* [C-002]. That is the reason you read it to them afterward.

So: write apart, then reveal. The two halves are doing different work.

Ninety days is long enough that you'll run out of the easy answers, which is the point. Around week
three you'll have to actually look at them.

# How to Use This Journal

Every day has two facing pages and one question.

The left page is **One**. The right page is **Two**. Decide now who is which, write it on the next
page, and keep it for the whole book. Your handwriting stays on one side the entire ninety days.

Answer your own side. Then stop.

Cover your side with your hand if you have to. It matters that you don't read theirs first.

If you read their answer before you write yours, you'll write toward it — agreeing, adding,
softening. That's a fine conversation and it isn't this one. Write what you actually saw. Then turn
the book around.

The reveal is the point. You'll find out what they noticed that you missed entirely.

**Read them out loud.** Not silently, sitting next to each other. Out loud, to their face. This
takes eleven seconds and it is the part most people skip. It's also the part with the research
behind it.

Three minutes each. Some nights it'll be two lines. That counts.

At the end of every week there's a page called **The Weekly Tether**. That one you do together, out
loud, both of you at once. Nothing to hide there.

There are no dates in this book. Start on a Tuesday in March. Miss four days and pick it up again.
A skipped page is a skipped page, not a failure — the only way to do this wrong is to stop.
