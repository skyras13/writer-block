# Voice Spec — Tether

> Binding contract. The Writer conforms, the Editor enforces, QA gates on it.
> In a journal, **every prompt is a piece of writing.** A weak prompt is a defect exactly like a
> weak paragraph.

- **Status:** approved
- **Created:** 2026-07-24

## 1. Reader and register

You are speaking to one person, alone, with a pen in their hand, about someone they love who is
probably in the next room. The relationship is not a peer's and not a guide's — it's the register
of a friend who knows what you mean and doesn't need you to explain. Warm, intimate, literary but
plain. Never sentimental, never clinical, never coaching. The book assumes the reader already loves
this person and has simply stopped saying why.

## 2. POV and tense

- **POV:** Second person. "You" is the writer; "they" is the partner.
- **Tense:** Present, or the immediate past ("this week," "the last time").
- **Address:** Direct but unhurried. Ask; never instruct about feelings.
- **The partner is always they/them.** No husband/wife, no he/she, no assumed marital status. Not a
  style choice — a content rule from `market/recommendation.md`.
- **Exception:** the front-matter essay "The Tether" may use "the two of you" and one first-person
  aside. Nowhere else.

## 3. Reading level

**Grade 8.** Concretely:
- Mostly one- and two-syllable words. A three-syllable word should be doing real work.
- One clause of subordination, rarely two. Never three.
- No jargon. No psychology vocabulary — not *attachment*, *validation*, *bid*, *responsiveness*,
  *emotional labor*, *love language*, *holding space*.
- No term needs defining, because no term needs using.

## 4. Sentence rhythm

**Prompts (the 90):**
- **8–22 words.** Under 8 is usually too thin to answer; over 22 is usually two questions.
- One question per prompt. If it needs "and," it's two prompts.
- Prompts may be a question, an instruction to describe, or a sentence stem. Vary the form — a
  run of five questions in a row is a defect.
- No prompt is answerable in one word. If "yes," "no," or a name finishes it, rewrite it.
- The specificity must be in the prompt, not asked for. "Describe the exact sound of their laugh
  from this week" — not "describe their laugh in detail."

**Theme epigraphs (13, one per week):**
- Two sentences. The first observes; the second turns it toward the reader.
- Under 25 words total. These are the most compressed writing in the book — every word earns.

**Front and back matter prose:**
- Average 12–16 words.
- **At least one sentence under six words per paragraph.** Short sentences carry the weight here.
- Paragraphs of 2–4 sentences. Never more than five.
- No three consecutive sentences with the same structure.
- Sections open on an image or a plain claim, never on setup.
- Sections close on the shortest sentence in the section.

## 5. Diction

### Embrace
noticing · small · quiet · ordinary · exact · the thing itself · hand · kitchen · Tuesday ·
doorway · pen · aloud · apart · reveal · read · watch · catch · keep · say it · out loud ·
the small print · line · thread · held · again · still · today

Concrete household nouns. Plain verbs. Specific days of the week. Bodies and rooms.

### Ban — Tether's own
Beyond the `CLAUDE.md` §7 list, these are banned in this book specifically:

*cherish · treasure · soulmate · better half · other half · significant other · magical · special ·
journey · spark · flame · rekindle · nurture · blessed · grateful heart · abundance · intentional
living · mindful (as a modifier) · connection (as an abstract noun) · communicate · validate ·
hold space · love language · attachment · vulnerability (as a noun) · authentic · deserve ·
"take a moment to" · "reflect on" · "think about a time when"*

Two hard rules embedded in that list:
- **Never "reflect on" or "think about a time when."** They are the two most common prompt-openers
  in this category and they are dead on the page. Ask the thing directly.
- **Never "connection."** The book is *about* it. Naming it is a substitute for showing it.

### Other diction rules
- **Contractions:** yes, throughout. Their absence sounds formal, and formal is fatal here.
- **Profanity:** none.
- **Figurative language:** sparingly — roughly one image per page of prose, none in most prompts.
  Drawn from **rooms, hands, light, thread, and weather in that order.**
- **Banned metaphor families:** journeys, war, sports, gardens, fire/flame, weaving-as-life,
  building-a-house. All are category clichés.
- **Em dashes:** allowed, at most one per paragraph.
- **No exclamation marks.** Anywhere.

## 6. Structural signature

- The book's title word appears in exactly three places: the title page, the front-matter essay,
  and the final weekly reflection. It is not sprinkled.
- Each week opens with a theme name and a two-sentence epigraph. Nothing else.
- Prompts are numbered 1–90, continuously, never restarting per week. The reader should feel the
  count accumulate.
- **"The Weekly Tether"** closes each week — one shared prompt, done aloud, on its own page.
- No takeaways. No summaries. No encouragement copy at week's end. The reader is an adult.
- Instructions appear once, in the front matter, and are never repeated.

## 7. Comparable authors

| author | what specifically we borrow | what is theirs alone |
|--------|----------------------------|----------------------|
| **Mary Oliver** | The instruction to pay attention to one ordinary thing, and the trust that the thing itself is enough — no interpretation added. | The natural world as subject; the line breaks; the prayer register. |
| **Raymond Carver** | Plain sentences about kitchens and marriages that don't announce what they mean. | The bleakness. Tether looks at the same rooms with warmth. |
| **Maggie Nelson** | Short numbered fragments that accumulate into an argument the reader assembles. | The theory, the citation, the intellectual register. |
| **Nora Ephron** | Specificity as affection — naming the exact gesture rather than the feeling. | The comic timing; the essayist's "I." |

Common thread: **the concrete detail is the emotion.** Never name the feeling; name what makes it.

## 8. Gold-standard samples

### Sample A — front-matter opening ("The Tether")

> A tether is the line that keeps two things joined when one of them drifts. It isn't decorative.
> You mostly don't see it. You notice it the day it isn't there.
>
> Most of what holds two people together is like that. It isn't the anniversary dinner. It's that
> they know how you take your coffee and they made it that way this morning without being asked,
> and you drank it without looking up.
>
> This journal is ninety days of looking up.

*Why this is the target:* Opens on an object, not an idea. Three sentences in the first paragraph,
and the third is six words. The second paragraph refuses the abstract ("what holds two people
together") and immediately replaces it with coffee. The close is one short sentence doing the work
a paragraph of explanation would do worse.

### Sample B — instruction ("How to Use This Journal")

> Cover your side with your hand if you have to. It matters that you don't read theirs first.
>
> If you read their answer before you write yours, you'll write toward it — agreeing, adding,
> softening. That's a fine conversation and it isn't this one. Write what you actually saw. Then
> turn the book around.
>
> The reveal is the point. You'll find out what they noticed that you missed entirely.

*Why this is the target:* Instructional prose without a single imperative about feelings. It gives
the reason, not just the rule — "you'll write toward it" is a specific, recognizable failure the
reader can picture. "Then turn the book around" is a physical action, not an emotional one. No
jargon anywhere; the whole passage is grade-6 vocabulary doing grade-12 work.

### Sample C — the intimate beat (a theme epigraph and prompt, Week 10)

> **Repair & Forgiveness**
> Lasting love is mostly mending. This week, notice the grace between you.
>
> 70. When did they love you at your least lovable?

*Why this is the target:* The epigraph is two sentences and eighteen words; the first observes, the
second turns. "Mostly mending" is a claim with an edge — a reader could disagree with it, which is
what keeps it from being a greeting card. The prompt is nine words, impossible to answer in one, and
asks for a specific memory rather than a feeling. It doesn't say "forgiveness"; it makes you go find
an instance of it.

## 9. Failure modes

| drift | the tell | the correction |
|-------|----------|----------------|
| **Saccharine** | Adjectives arrive before nouns. *Beautiful, precious, wonderful.* The prompt tells the reader how to feel about the answer before they've written it. | Delete the adjective. Ask for the object. "What is one small thing" beats "what is one beautiful little thing." |
| **Therapy-speak** | *Space, process, hold, honor, validate, show up for.* Sounds caring, means nothing, and implies clinical competence we aren't claiming. | Replace with a physical verb. Not "how did they hold space for you" — "when did they sit there and let you finish." |
| **Vague** | "Think about a time when…" · "Reflect on how…" · "In what ways does…" | Ask for one specific instance with a time bound: *this week, today, the last time.* |
| **Interview-ish** | Prompts start sounding like a questionnaire. Five questions in a row, all starting "What." | Vary the form — a description, a sentence stem, a "finish this." Three same-shaped prompts in a row is a defect. |
| **Preachy** | The book starts telling the reader what love is, or what they should do about it. Second person turns into instruction. | Cut it. The book asks; it does not teach. If a sentence explains the value of the exercise, delete the sentence. |
