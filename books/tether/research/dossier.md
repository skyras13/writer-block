# Research Dossier — Tether

**Researcher pass:** 1 · **Date:** 2026-07-24 · **Citations:** `../citations.md`

---

## Scope

Tether is a fill-in journal. **Ninety of its ~215 pages ask questions and assert nothing.** The
entire factual surface of this book is one front-matter essay, "The Tether," roughly 400 words.

That is unusual, and it changes the job. Instead of supporting an argument across twenty chapters,
the research has to do exactly two things:

1. Justify the **write-apart** step.
2. Justify the **reveal-and-read-aloud** step.

If the evidence supported neither, the mechanic would be a nice idea with nothing behind it and the
front matter would have to say so. It supports both, specifically, with primary sources.

## By manuscript section

### Front matter — "The Tether" (why this journal exists)

**What it needs to establish**
- Small, unremarked acts are what actually hold two people together.
- Writing about the relationship — privately — is worth doing.
- Saying it out loud to the other person is worth doing, and is a separate act from writing it.

**What the evidence actually supports**

| the claim the book makes | the evidence | scaled correctly? |
|---|---|---|
| Feeling appreciated and being appreciative travel together with responsiveness and commitment | [C-001] Gordon et al. 2012, *JPSP* 103(2) — multi-method, incl. longitudinal | ✅ stated as association, never as cause |
| Writing privately about a relationship is linked to still being together later | [C-003] Slatcher & Pennebaker 2006, *Psych Science* 17(8) — randomized writing condition, n=86 dating couples, 3-month follow-up | ✅ named as **one study**, with its size, in the text |
| Expressing gratitude *aloud* to a partner predicts relationship quality six months on | [C-002] Algoe, Fredrickson & Gable 2013, *Emotion* 13(4) — lab expression task, 6-month follow-up | ✅ this is the citation for the reveal step specifically |

**Where the evidence is thin — and it is worth being blunt about this**

- [C-003] is **one study, twenty years old, on 86 dating couples, followed for three months.** It is
  the closest thing in the literature to "writing apart about your relationship helps," and it is
  not much. The manuscript cites it as one study and gives the number 86 in the text, so the reader
  can weigh it themselves. It does not say "research shows."
- [C-001] is correlational and longitudinal. It cannot carry a causal claim, and the front matter
  doesn't ask it to.
- [C-002] measures *perceived responsiveness after an expression task*, not "reading a journal entry
  aloud." The mechanism is adjacent to ours, not identical. The book's phrasing stays at the level
  the study supports: saying it out loud to the person is doing something different from thinking it.
- **No study anywhere supports "a 90-day journal will improve your relationship."** Nothing in the
  book says it will.

**Surprising finding that changed the design**

[C-002] and [C-003] are evidence for two *different* acts. Writing privately is one intervention;
expressing aloud is another. Most products in this category do one — a shared page where you write
and it's simultaneously the sharing. Tether's mechanic is the only one in the scan that does both
as separate steps, and it turns out that's the shape the evidence actually has. That's a stronger
argument for the mechanic than the market analysis alone, and the front matter uses it.

**What I could not verify**
- The full text of [C-002] (paywalled; citation confirmed against the first author's own
  publications page, findings confirmed across independent summaries).
- The full text of [C-005] (Wiley returns 403). Held as background, **not cited in the book.**

### The 90 prompts and 13 weekly reflections

**Research needed:** none. They make no factual claims.

**What research does constrain here:** the ban on therapy vocabulary in the voice spec is partly a
research-integrity rule. Words like *responsiveness*, *attachment*, and *validation* are technical
terms with specific meanings in the literature. Using them casually in a prompt would imply the
book is applying findings it isn't.

### Back matter — "A keepsake"

The Tether app connection is documented first-party [C-004]: the site describes itself as "A Shared
Journal for Two" and states its mechanic as *"You write alone. When both of you have answered, the
words unlock side-by-side."* Cited so the self-adaptation is explicit rather than implied.

## Claims that must be cut or softened

Handed to the Writer as a hard list. All three were considered and all three are **out**.

1. **The Gottman "86% vs 33%" bids statistic — CUT.**
   It is the single most quotable number in relationship science and it belongs in a book like this.
   It is real and attributable to the Gottman Institute (gottman.com, Zach Brittle, 2015-04-01), but
   no peer-reviewed source giving the sample size or study was locatable. That makes it a laundered
   number, however famous. Cut entirely. Nothing replaces it.

2. **"77% of participants were still dating three months later" — CUT.**
   Appears in 2006 secondary coverage of [C-003] but not in the PubMed abstract, and the paywalled
   full text could not confirm it. Logged as [C-006]. [C-003] makes its point without a percentage.

3. **Any causal phrasing — SOFTENED.**
   Not "gratitude makes relationships last." Not "this practice will bring you closer." The front
   matter names studies, gives their size, and stops. Every instance was rewritten.

**Also cut:** all Amazon prices, ratings, and review counts from the market scan (product pages
return HTTP 500 to automated requests). Left blank, not estimated. They appear nowhere in the book
and support no argument in the brief.

## Currency flags

| source | age at publication | flag |
|--------|-------------------|------|
| [C-003] Slatcher & Pennebaker | 2006 → **20 years** | ⚠️ Flagged. Cited as a specific dated study, not as current consensus. Still the most-cited work on expressive writing in couples; no replication or refutation surfaced in this scan. |
| [C-001] Gordon et al. | 2012 → 14 years | ⚠️ Noted. Widely cited and not contradicted in this scan. |
| [C-002] Algoe et al. | 2013 → 13 years | ⚠️ Noted. Consistent with the author's continuing work through at least 2023. |

None of these is fast-moving science. A 2006 finding about expressive writing does not decay the
way a 2006 finding about, say, screen time would. But the ages are on the record, and the book cites
by name and year so a reader can go look.
